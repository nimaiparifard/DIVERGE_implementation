---------------------------------------------------------------------
0. THE REVIEW COMMENT, SPLIT INTO 4 PARTS
---------------------------------------------------------------------

R2-C5: "The proposed adaptive ensemble module introduces additional
learnable parameters, but the paper does not provide sufficient analysis
on how the learned weights behave or why they improve over simple
averaging."

This one sentence asks 4 things. Each experiment answers one of them:

  Part 1  DO they improve?   Is learned > simple averaging, under a fair
                             protocol, and is it significant?   -> Exp3 (+Exp1)
  Part 2  HOW do they behave? What do the weights look like, do they
                             react to a bad view, per class, per node?
                                                    -> Exp2, Exp6, Exp5 (+stability)
  Part 3  WHY / WHEN do they improve?  Where does the gain come from, and
                             why is it small in some cases? -> Exp1, Exp7, Exp9
  Part 4  ARE THE EXTRA PARAMETERS WORTH IT?  What do 5 / 35 / ~5000
                             parameters buy vs 0-parameter rules? -> Exp3

Words used below:
  uniform   = simple averaging of the 5 views' logits (0 parameters).
  scalar    = one learned weight per view (5 params).
  per-class = one learned weight per view per class (35 Cora, 30 CiteSeer).
  node-MLP  = small MLP that gives each node its own weights (~2-8k params).

  protocol "paper"     = head fit on train nodes, epoch/config picked on
                         the TEST nodes (what the current paper code does).
                         -> optimistic, test is used for selection.
  protocol "val_split" = head fit on half of val, picked on the other half,
                         test used once. -> the CLEAN number.
  protocol "train_fit" = fit on train, pick on val. Clean, but the head
                         learns from over-confident train logits.

2a. Exp2 - break one view on purpose (exp2_weak_view_injection.py)

Simple explanation
  Part A: take the best view, damage its LLM embeddings (add noise of
  growing size, shuffle the rows so text and node do not match, replace
  with random numbers, or use the base LLM without fine-tuning), retrain
  its GNN, refit the heads. Watch: (1) the weight the head gives that
  view, (2) the ensemble accuracy.
  Part B: copy the weakest view 0..4 extra times. Simple averaging then
  gives that view more and more of the vote (0.20 -> 0.56). A good learned
  head should notice the copies add nothing new.

3b. Exp7 - is the gain just calibration? (exp7_logit_scale_calibration.py)

Simple explanation
  Averaging raw logits is only a fair vote if all views have the same
  logit size. If one view has bigger logits, it secretly gets more vote.
  We measure each view's "real share" of the uniform average, and check
  how much of the learned gain a 5-number temperature fix also gets.

Results
                    real share per view   temp. scaling recovers   rho(weight,1/T)
                    (fair = 0.200)        of scalar / MLP gain
  Cora LLaMA        0.180-0.208           43% / 21%                +0.63
  Cora SmolLM2      0.176-0.255 (Orth)    20% / 19%                +0.13
  Cora Gemma2       0.183-0.211           (gain ~0)                +0.68
  CiteSeer LLaMA    0.145 (Orth)-0.222    48% / 37%                +0.78
  ECE of learned heads 0.039-0.053, not better than uniform probs /
  temperature-scaled averaging (0.031-0.047).

  Reading: 20-50% of the scalar gain is just re-scaling the views'
  confidence (a temperature). The learned heads do not improve calibration.

3c. Exp9 - how much does each view contribute? (exp9_view_contribution.py)

Simple explanation
  Run the ensemble on all 31 subsets of the 5 views. From this compute
  each view's Shapley value (its fair share of the ensemble accuracy) and
  the drop when it is removed. Then check if the learned weights follow
  these contributions.

Results (uniform averaging)
                    Shapley range (pp)      leave-one-out    rho(weight,
                                            drop (pp)        Shapley)
  Cora LLaMA        10.75 (Orth) - 12.40    -0.65 .. +0.18   +0.53
  Cora SmolLM2      11.61 - 12.49           -0.28 .. +0.18   +0.18
  Cora Gemma2       11.68 - 12.57           -0.05 .. +0.51   +0.14
  CiteSeer LLaMA    -4.14 (Orth) .. +16.37  -0.82 .. +1.96   +0.70
  CiteSeer LLaMA with the learned scalar head: Orthogonal Shapley goes from
  -4.14 (it HURTS the average) to +0.31 (neutral).

  Reading: on Cora the 5 views contribute almost equally. If all views are
  worth the same, equal weights are already the right weights, so a learned
  head has nothing to fix. On CiteSeer/LLaMA one view is harmful, and the
  learned weights follow the contributions (rho +0.70) and neutralise it.

Answer to Part 3, in one sentence
  The learned weights help when the views are UNBALANCED (one weak,
  harmful, over-confident or duplicated view): they down-weight it and
  remove its damage. When the views are balanced (Cora: equal Shapley
  values, ~equal logit scale, ~89% unanimous nodes) the best weights are
  ~uniform, so the learned head cannot gain much and only adds noise.

---------------------------------------------------------------------
5. ONLY THE STRONG RESULTS UNDER A CLEAN PROTOCOL (use these in the paper)
---------------------------------------------------------------------

The reviewer does not ask for a specific experiment, only for "sufficient
analysis" of (a) the extra parameters, (b) how the weights behave, (c) why
they help. So we report only results that are CLEAN and STRONG.

A result is CLEAN here if either
  - no learned head is involved (nothing can be selected on test:
    uniform averaging, oracle, Shapley, logit scales), or
  - the head is fitted on half of the validation nodes, selected on the
    other half, and the test nodes are used once (protocol val_split).
Everything under the 'paper' protocol (epoch picked on test) is left out.

Significance: paired tests of method A minus method B over the runs.
"seed p" = test on the 4 GNN-seed means (conservative); "pooled" = the 16
GNN-seed means of all 4 settings (Cora x 3 LLMs + CiteSeer LLaMA).


RESULT 1 - The extra parameters are safe because the weights are
           CONSTRAINED (answers "additional learnable parameters")
           Exp3, val_split
---------------------------------------------------------------------
  Simple idea: give a combiner extra parameters but NO constraint
  (stacking = logistic regression on all view logits, 186-252 params),
  and compare it with DIVERGE's convex (softmax) weights (5 / 30-35 params),
  trained on exactly the same nodes.

                     per-class - stacking   scalar - stacking   W/L (per-class)
  Cora LLaMA            +0.94 pp              +0.83 pp            11/1
  Cora SmolLM2          +1.20 pp              +1.17 pp            11/1
  Cora Gemma2           +1.15 pp              +1.23 pp            11/1
  CiteSeer LLaMA        +1.41 pp              +1.07 pp            12/0
  Pooled (16 seeds)     +1.18 pp, 16/16 positive, t p = 2.5e-6, Wilcoxon p = 4e-4
                        scalar: +1.07 pp, 15/16, t p = 3.1e-6

  Stacking is BELOW simple averaging on all 3 Cora LLMs (-0.43, -1.05,
  -1.23 pp); the convex heads are not.
  -> SIGNIFICANT. The constraint is what makes the extra parameters
     harmless: an unconstrained learned combiner overfits, DIVERGE's
     softmax weights do not.

---------------------------------------------------------------------
  "Additional learnable parameters" -> Result 1 + 2: the 5-35 extra
  parameters are constrained to a convex combination; they never hurt
  accuracy on balanced views, while an unconstrained combiner with the
  same data loses up to 1.2 pp (16/16 seeds, p < 1e-5).

6. WHAT TO CLAIM IN THE PAPER (honest framing that the data supports)
---------------------------------------------------------------------

  Do NOT claim: "trainable ensemble weights significantly improve over
  simple averaging." Under a clean protocol this is not true on balanced
  views, and the current numbers use the test set for selection.

  DO claim:
  (1) The multi-view ensemble itself has headroom: the oracle is +3.4 to
      +9 pp above uniform, so the views are complementary.
  (2) The learned weights are a SAFEGUARD: they detect and down-weight a
      broken, untuned or redundant view (weights 0.02-0.13), which simple
      averaging cannot do (it loses up to 16-25 pp in these cases). This
      happened naturally on CiteSeer/LLaMA, where one LoRA init collapsed.
  (3) On balanced views the optimal weights are close to uniform, and we
      show WHY: ~89% of nodes are unanimous, views have nearly equal
      Shapley values and logit scales. So learned and uniform perform on
      par there (within +/-0.5 pp).
  (4) Small constrained heads (scalar / per-class, 5-35 params) are enough;
      an unconstrained combiner (stacking) overfits.

  Mandatory fix: the paper's ensemble numbers must be recomputed with a
  clean protocol (val_split), because the current code picks the epoch
  and config on the test set (see explain_ensemble_experiment.txt,
  section 1). The revised text already says validation is used; the
  numbers must match it.

7. DRAFT RESPONSE TO R2-C5
---------------------------------------------------------------------

  We thank the reviewer. We added a dedicated analysis of the adaptive
  ensemble (Section X, Appendix Y) on Cora (three LLMs) and CiteSeer. All
  combining rules are applied to exactly the same GNNs; the ensemble head
  is fitted and selected on validation nodes only.
  (i) Do the learned weights improve over averaging? With balanced views
  (Cora), the learned heads perform on par with uniform averaging
  (-0.1 to +0.5 pp, not significant), while with an unbalanced pool
  (CiteSeer, where one LoRA initialisation produced a weak view) the
  class-level head improves uniform averaging by +1.6 pp.
  (ii) How do the weights behave? When a view is corrupted (shuffled
  features or base-model embeddings) or duplicated, its learned weight
  falls to 0.02-0.13, while uniform averaging keeps giving it 20-56% of
  the vote and loses up to 16 pp accuracy (CiteSeer duplication: 79.2 ->
  62.8 for uniform vs 81.9 for the learned head). Class-level weights
  correlate with per-class view accuracy (Spearman +0.51 to +0.60).
  (iii) Why is the improvement small on balanced views? 88-89% of Cora test
  nodes receive the same prediction from all views, so no weighting can
  change them; the views have almost equal Shapley contributions
  (11.6-12.6 pp) and similar logit scales, so uniform weights are already
  near-optimal. We have revised our claim accordingly: the trainable
  weights act as a low-cost safeguard (5-35 parameters) that removes weak
  or redundant views automatically, and their benefit grows with how
  unbalanced the views are.
